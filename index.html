Project - Urban Heat Island Dynamics: Spatial Analysis and Predictive Simulation Modelling

This graph displays the datasets, highlighting the impact of the Urban Heat Island (UHI) effect. The graph clearly shows that temperatures have increased over time in both areas; however, the average temperature in Lucknow city is consistently higher than in the peripheral regions. This indicates the presence of the UHI effect. If we analyse the pattern, it is apparent that in the early years, the temperatures in the city and surrounding areas were more similar. However, after 2000, the city’s temperature became consistently higher, reflecting increased urbanization, reduced vegetation, and significant land use changes. Furthermore, the growing gap between the average temperatures of urban and rural areas suggests that factors like global warming are contributing to rising temperatures overall. This, in turn, intensifies the UHI effect and could potentially lead to more frequent and severe heat waves in the region. The trend clearly shows an upward trajectory, reinforcing the concern over rising urban temperatures.

To validate whether there is an actual trend in the previous graph, we applied the Mann-Kendall test to determine if the trend is increasing, decreasing, or non-existent. Additionally, we used Sen’s slope estimation to assess the magnitude of change over time. In the given graph, we observe a positive Tau value of 0.06. Although this indicates a weak upward trend, it is very close to zero and not statistically significant. Since the p-value is 0.551, which is greater than the commonly used significance level of 0.05, we conclude that the trend is not statistically significant. This means we fail to reject the null hypothesis, which states that there is no trend, and we do not accept the alternative hypothesis of a significant trend. The estimated magnitude of change over the last 44 years is 0.006°C for Lucknow city and 0.007°C for the peripheral region. Observing the entire graph, we notice considerable interannual variability, particularly after 2000. Prominent temperature spikes are observed around 2009, 2016, and 2023, possibly related to extreme weather events or El Niño years. Conversely, lower temperature clusters appear in the early 1980s and around 2020, which may be associated with La Niña or other cooling events.

The given graph clearly illustrates the relationship between rainfall and Urban Heat Island Intensity (UHII), showing an inverse correlation between the two datasets. As rainfall decreases, UHII tends to increase, indicating that reduced precipitation may exacerbate urban warming.

From the graph, it is evident that UHII has been increasing steadily since around 2005. Notably, peak UHII values occurred in the early 1990s, and again around 2015 and 2018, suggesting stronger urban heat effects during those monsoon years. This implies that even during traditionally cooler, rainier seasons, urban areas are experiencing elevated heat levels—possibly due to intensified urbanization and declining vegetation. However, in the past five years, there appears to be a decline or stabilization in UHII values. This trend may point to a temporary reduction in UHI intensity, potentially due to land use changes, increased green cover, or other climate adaptation measures. Overall, the observed pattern underscores the urgent need for climate-resilient urban planning and effective heat mitigation strategies, especially in monsoon-dependent regions.

Project - Comfort Zone Map of Lucknow City (1999-2025)

The "Comfort Zone Map of Lucknow City" illustrates the spatial and temporal changes in thermal comfort levels across the city over four distinct years: 1999, 2009, 2017, and 2025 (projected). The maps use a detailed color-coded legend ranging from "Very High Comfort Zone" (deep green) to "Extremely Discomfort Zone" (deep red), helping visualize shifts in environmental conditions over time.

  • 1999: A Predominantly Comfortable City
    In 1999, the city enjoys widespread thermal comfort. Green and light green zones (moderate to high comfort) dominate the landscape, especially in the central, southern, and eastern regions. Discomfort zones are limited and fragmented, suggesting a healthy urban ecosystem with ample vegetation, low-density development, and effective natural ventilation. This year represents a baseline of ecological balance, where the built environment and natural systems coexist relatively harmoniously.

  • 2009: Early Signs of Urban Thermal Stress
    By 2009, a noticeable shift occurs: Orange and red zones (discomfort and high discomfort) begin to replace the earlier comfort zones, especially in the central and northern parts of the city. The moderately comfortable areas shrink, indicating growing heat stress from increasing built-up surfaces, declining green cover, and intensifying anthropogenic heat. This period marks the onset of the urban heat island effect, a by-product of rapid, often unplanned, urban expansion.

  • 2017: Intensification of Discomfort
    The year 2017 shows a concerning transformation. Red zones (extremely discomfort) now dominate vast portions of the city, with the discomfort belt spreading widely across northern and central areas. Comfort zones have become isolated patches, confined mainly to the southern and peripheral zones. The map suggests an urgent environmental tipping point, where thermal discomfort has become a citywide norm rather than an exception.

  • 2025 (Projected): A City Under Severe Thermal Stress
    The projection for 2025 reveals an alarming transformation. The majority of the city falls under extremely discomfort and high discomfort zones, with red and orange hues overwhelming the map. Only a few fragments of comfort zones remain, mostly on the urban fringe, indicating significant ecological degradation. This scenario forecasts a future of widespread thermal discomfort, posing serious risks to public health, energy consumption, and liveability if current trends persist.

Conclusion: A Call for Sustainable Urban Resilience

The maps collectively depict a clear and troubling trajectory—from a largely comfortable urban environment to one dominated by heat stress and discomfort. This trend correlates strongly with:

  1. Urban sprawl
  2. Loss of vegetation and open spaces
  3. Rising surface temperatures
  4. Increased infrastructure density

To reverse or mitigate these effects, urgent policy action is required. Recommendations include:

  1. Enhancing urban green infrastructure (parks, green roofs, vertical gardens)
  2. Promoting climate-sensitive urban design
  3. Preserving and expanding blue and green networks
  4. Implementing heat-resilient architecture and materials

In essence, the comfort zone maps of Lucknow are more than just thematic representations—they are visual warnings and planning guides for building a more resilient, sustainable, and liveable city.

Project - Impact of Climate Change on Rainfall Variability in Bhima Basin

The Precipitation Concentration Index (PCI) across the basin, with values remaining consistently elevated throughout the entire study area. All 49 monitoring stations exceeded the baseline threshold of 21, placing the entire region firmly within the very high rainfall concentration category.

This indicates that precipitation is delivered highly unevenly over time. Station 999 recorded the highest PCI value in the dataset (24.79), followed closely by Stations 865 and 909. Such intense temporal concentration directly contributes to hydrological extremes, increasing the risk of flash flooding, prolonging dry periods, and accelerating soil erosion through rapid runoff generation.

Stations 867, 826, and 827 recorded the lowest PCI values in the dataset; however, even these minimums remain well above the severe concentration threshold, confirming that well-distributed rainfall is entirely absent from the basin.

Spatially, the central basin dominates the distribution, falling within a PCI range of 24.07–24.77, represented by light green shading on the map. The highest PCI values are concentrated in the north-western sector, indicated by darker shading, marking a localized zone of peak rainfall concentration.

The Percent of Normal Precipitation Index (PNPI) is a straightforward measure that compares actual rainfall at a station to its long-term average (or "normal") rainfall, expressed as a percentage. Values above 100 indicate wetter-than-normal conditions, while values below 100 indicate drier-than-normal conditions, allowing stations to be classified into categories ranging from severe drought to humid. The PNPI analysis reveals that out of the 49 stations, 29 are classified as humid, with PNPI values exceeding 120, indicating a prevalence of above-average rainfall conditions at these locations. Station 865 recorded the highest PNPI value in the dataset. Five stations fall within the normal category, with PNPI values ranging from 80 to 120. Station 827 falls under the light drought category, while 12 stations are classified under moderate drought, and two stations are categorized as experiencing severe drought conditions.

Above-average rainfall carries both benefits and risks. On the positive side, it can improve water quality, enhance crop yields, support aquifer recharge, and reduce the risk of drought. Conversely, excessive rainfall may lead to flooding, soil erosion, and landslides, while also posing challenges for agricultural activities and transportation.

In terms of spatial distribution, the map shows that the north-western part of the basin records the highest PNPI values, represented in blue shades, while the lowest values are concentrated in the red-shaded areas.

The Heat map presents monthly rainfall (in mm) for a zone of the basin where the average annual rainfall ranges between 100 and 150 cm, covering the period from 1971 to 2022. For most of the record, rainfall remained largely confined to the monsoon months (June–September), with values generally concentrated between 0 and 500 mm, while the remaining months stayed close to zero.

This pattern shifted notably around 2005, when monsoon rainfall intensified sharply. July 2005 recorded the highest rainfall in the entire dataset, at approximately 2,500 mm, with elevated values also observed in the adjoining months of June and August (1,500–2,000 mm) and September (around 1,000 mm). This anomalous spike coincided with a major flood event in the region. Similar, though less extreme, elevated rainfall was also observed during the monsoon seasons of 2009 and 2011, suggesting a broader trend of intensifying monsoon rainfall in the years following 2005.

Project - PMKVY Spatial Analysis in Jharkhand

Geospatial investigation of skill-development centres, accessibility, terrain and spatial patterns among tribal youth.

In spatial analysis and environmental modelling, Slope and Terrain Ruggedness Index (TRI) are critical topographic metrics used to evaluate landform geometry, accessibility, and surface movement. Slope measures the rate of elevation change (steepness) in degrees or percentage, making it essential for assessing slope stability, soil erosion potential, landslides, surface runoff velocity, and land-use suitability for agriculture and urban development. In contrast, TRI quantifies elevation variation among neighboring grid cells to represent surface roughness; high TRI values indicate jagged, broken, or complex terrain, whereas low values signify smooth, flat topography. Together, these metrics are extensively applied in hydrological modelling, infrastructure planning, route optimization, ecological habitat suitability mapping, and spatial access analysis—such as identifying physical barriers to transport, healthcare, or educational facilities in rugged geographic regions.

Combining Elevation (a Digital Elevation Model or DEM) and Hillshade into a single overlay creates a depth-enhanced, realistic visual representation of landscape topography that bridges quantitative elevation data with intuitive spatial perception. In this technique, a semi-transparent, color-coded elevation layer is draped over a grayscale hillshade raster—which simulates directional sunlight and shadow across terrain—to clearly accentuate landform features like valleys, ridges, drainage channels, and steep escarpments. Beyond cartographic visual enhancement, this combined analytical tool is extensively used in geomorphology, hydrological watershed delineation, natural hazard assessment (such as mapping landslide susceptibility and flood pathways), and infrastructure routing. Furthermore, by pairing absolute height above sea level with surface illumination geometry, it allows researchers to accurately model microclimates, solar radiation exposure, and physical accessibility across complex terrain.

Project - Comparative Analysis of Indian Summer Monsoon Rainfall in Various Observational/Reanalysis Datasets

The Mann-Kendall trend test was applied to the annual rainfall series under six configurations: the unmodified record ("All Years"), two outlier-filtered versions (excluding observations beyond 1.5σ and 2σ from the mean, respectively), and the same three configurations following a systematic +1° adjustment.

For the unadjusted series, the full record exhibited a statistically significant declining trend (τ = −0.15, p = 0.022). However, this trend was not robust to outlier removal: after excluding values beyond 1.5σ, no significant trend remained (τ = −0.00, p = 0.951), and after 2σ filtering the trend was likewise non-significant and weakly positive (τ = 0.12, p = 0.072). This indicates that the apparent decline in the raw series is disproportionately influenced by a small number of extreme high- and low-rainfall years rather than reflecting a consistent monotonic shift across the record.

In contrast, the trend was considerably more robust in the 1°-adjusted series. All three variants showed a negative association, with the strongest and most significant decline observed after 1.5σ filtering (τ = −0.26, p = 0.0001), followed by the unfiltered adjusted series (τ = −0.24, p = 0.0002). The 2σ-filtered adjusted series approached but did not reach conventional significance (τ = −0.13, p = 0.056). The persistence of a significant declining trend even after removal of extreme values suggests that the 1° adjustment captures a systematic component of the decline that is not attributable solely to outlier influence, and which becomes more apparent in the latter half of the record (post-1980).

Taken together, these results suggest that evidence for a long-term decline in rainfall over the study period is sensitive to both the treatment of extreme values and the applied adjustment. While the unadjusted data alone provide only weak and outlier-dependent support for a declining trend, the adjusted series point toward a more robust and statistically defensible decline, warranting further investigation into the physical basis of the adjustment and its interaction with interannual variability.
